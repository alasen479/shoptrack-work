How to Manage Cash Flow in Your Small Business in Cameroon

A business can be profitable on paper and still run out of cash. Cash flow problems — not lack of customers — are the number one reason small businesses in Cameroon close. Here is how to understand, track, and control yours every week.

There is an important distinction that many small business owners in Cameroon never fully grasp: the difference between profit and cash flow. Profit is what remains after you subtract your costs from your revenue — it is an accounting concept. Cash flow is what is actually in your account or cash box right now, available to pay your suppliers, your rent, your staff, and your personal salary. A business can show a profit on paper and simultaneously be unable to pay a supplier because the cash is not there.

This gap between profit and cash is where most Cameroonian small businesses get into trouble. Sales are recorded, costs are deducted, a profit appears — but much of that profit is locked up in unpaid customer credit, in stock that has not yet been sold, or in a timing mismatch between when money goes out and when it comes in. Managing cash flow means actively managing this gap, every week, before it becomes a crisis.

What cash flow actually means for a small business

Cash flow is the movement of money in and out of your business over a period of time. Positive cash flow means more money is coming in than going out. Negative cash flow means more is going out than coming in. Sustained negative cash flow — even for a few weeks — can force a business to close regardless of how good its products or its reputation are.

For a small business in Cameroon, cash inflows typically include: daily sales revenue in cash or Mobile Money, repayments of customer credit, and any loans or external funding received. Cash outflows include: stock purchases, rent, staff wages, utilities, transport, loan repayments, and personal withdrawals by the owner.

The critical insight is that cash flow is about timing, not just amounts. If you need to pay your supplier 150,000 XAF on Monday but your biggest customer only pays their credit on Friday, you have a cash flow problem on Monday — even if your business is technically profitable that week.

The five most common cash flow problems in Cameroonian SMEs

1. Uncontrolled customer credit

Selling on credit is common and often necessary in Cameroon. But when credit is extended without a clear tracking system and without firm repayment terms, it quietly converts sales into unpaid promises. A business doing 500,000 XAF per month in sales may have 150,000 XAF of that tied up in outstanding customer credit at any given time — money that appears in revenue calculations but cannot pay a single bill.

The solution is not to stop selling on credit. It is to record every credit sale immediately, set a clear repayment date, and follow up professionally before the debt goes cold. 2. Owner withdrawals with no fixed salary

When a business owner takes money from the business whenever they need it personally — paying school fees, buying food, covering a family commitment — without any structure or limit, the business's cash position erodes unpredictably. Some months the withdrawals are modest. Others they are enormous. Without a fixed, budgeted salary, there is no way to plan around the owner's personal cash needs.

The fix is simple and non-negotiable: pay yourself a fixed monthly salary. Decide what you need to live, make it a line item in your expenses, and take only that amount — consistently, on the same date each month. Everything else stays in the business.

3. Overstocking

Buying too much stock locks cash inside merchandise that is sitting on shelves. The stock has value — but you cannot pay rent with stock. Every franc invested in slow-moving inventory is a franc unavailable for operational expenses. This is particularly acute in retail and food businesses in Cameroon, where the temptation to buy in bulk for a better unit price regularly creates short-term cash crunches.

4. No cash reserve

Operating without any financial cushion means that any unexpected event — a supplier who demands immediate payment, an equipment breakdown, a slow week, a medical emergency — immediately threatens the business's ability to operate. A cash reserve of even two to three weeks of operating expenses provides enough buffer to absorb most common shocks without crisis.

5. Mixing cash flows from multiple revenue streams

Some business owners in Cameroon run multiple activities — a shop, a side service, a rental property — and pool all the cash together. When one activity loses money, it silently drains the others. Without clear separation, there is no way to know which activity is healthy and which one is consuming the collective cash.

How to track your cash flow every week

Cash flow management does not require complex accounting software or a trained accountant. It requires one habit: knowing your cash position at the start and end of every week, and understanding why it changed.

At the start of each week, record:

  • Total cash in hand (physical notes and coins in your cash box)
  • Total balance across all Mobile Money accounts used for the business
  • Total outstanding customer credit (what customers owe you)
  • Total outstanding supplier debt (what you owe suppliers)

At the end of each week, record the same four figures again. The change in each tells you exactly what happened to your money that week. If your cash in hand dropped but your customer credit also dropped, money moved from credit into cash — healthy. If your cash in hand dropped and your supplier debt increased, you spent money you did not have — a warning signal.

Building a simple cash flow forecast

A cash flow forecast is a prediction of your cash position over the coming weeks. It does not need to be elaborate. For a small business in Cameroon, a simple weekly forecast answers three questions:

  1. How much cash do I expect to receive this week? (Estimated sales + expected credit repayments)
  2. How much cash do I expect to pay out this week? (Scheduled expenses + planned stock purchases + salary + rent if due)
  3. What will my cash balance be at the end of the week? (Opening balance + expected receipts − expected payments)

If the projected end balance is negative or dangerously low, you know in advance — and you have time to act. You can delay a non-urgent stock purchase, chase an outstanding credit payment, or arrange a short-term solution. Forecasting turns cash flow management from reactive firefighting into deliberate planning.

Practical strategies to improve your cash flow

Collect credit faster

Your fastest and most direct lever for improving cash flow is recovering outstanding customer credit. Go through your credit list and contact every customer with an outstanding balance. Prioritise the largest amounts and the oldest debts first. Even recovering half of outstanding credit in a single week can transform a tight cash position. ShopTrack shows you every outstanding debt ranked by amount, so you know exactly where to start.

Negotiate better payment terms with suppliers

Many Cameroonian small business owners accept whatever payment terms their suppliers offer without negotiation. If you have a good relationship with a supplier and consistent purchase history, there is often room to negotiate: paying 50% upfront and 50% in 15 days instead of 100% upfront, or getting 7-day credit on regular orders. Each day of additional supplier credit is a day your cash stays in your account.

Create a minimum cash reserve

Set a specific amount — even 30,000 or 50,000 XAF — that you will never let your business cash balance fall below. Treat this as untouchable except in genuine emergencies. When your balance approaches this floor, it triggers immediate action: chase credit, reduce discretionary spending, accelerate a sale. This floor prevents the gradual drift into a crisis that catches most business owners by surprise.

Reduce slow-moving stock

Identify the items in your stock that have not moved in 45 days or more. Run a promotion, reduce the price, or offer a bundle with a faster-moving item. The goal is not to maximise profit on these items — it is to convert stagnant stock back into cash that can be redeployed into items that turn faster.

Cash flow is not an accounting problem. It is a management problem. The businesses that solve it are not the ones with the most revenue — they are the ones that know where their money is at all times.

The weekly cash flow habit — a simple routine

Every Sunday evening or Monday morning, spend ten minutes on your cash position:

  • Count your physical cash and check your Mobile Money balance
  • Review your ShopTrack dashboard — total sales this week, total expenses, outstanding customer credit
  • Identify any credit repayments expected this week and plan to follow up on them
  • List any large payments due this week — rent, supplier orders, wages
  • Confirm your balance covers everything due, with your minimum reserve intact

This ten-minute weekly routine catches problems while they are still manageable. Without it, most business owners only discover a cash problem when they are already in it.

According to the World Bank's SME Finance research, cash flow mismanagement — not market conditions — accounts for over 65% of small business closures in Sub-Saharan Africa. The INS Cameroun business surveys consistently identify trésorerie (cash flow) as the number one operational challenge reported by Cameroonian SME owners across all sectors. Research published in Frontiers in Business and Management confirms that SMEs which implement weekly cash flow monitoring reduce their risk of acute liquidity crisis by over 50% compared to those that review finances only at month-end.

The bottom line: Cash flow is the oxygen of your business. You can survive a bad week of sales. You cannot survive running out of cash.
